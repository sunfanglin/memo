# Possible Future Work on Lake-Atmosphere Interaction

This document lists possible future research directions in lake-atmosphere interaction, with emphasis on in situ observations, model simulation, and observation-model integration.

## 1. Scientific Motivation

Lakes strongly regulate regional weather, boundary-layer development, water and energy cycles, and local climate. They exchange heat, moisture, momentum, carbon dioxide, methane, aerosols, and other materials with the atmosphere. These exchanges are especially important in regions with large lake clusters, high-altitude lakes, seasonally frozen lakes, rapidly warming climates, or strong land-lake thermal contrast.

Future work should move beyond treating lakes as passive lower-boundary conditions and instead examine them as active components of the coupled land-lake-atmosphere system.

## 2. Key Scientific Questions

1. How do lake surface temperature, mixed-layer depth, stratification, and ice cover regulate sensible heat, latent heat, and momentum fluxes?
2. How do lakes modify atmospheric boundary-layer height, stability, turbulence, cloud formation, precipitation, and local circulation?
3. What is the relative importance of lake size, depth, salinity, water clarity, surrounding terrain, and seasonal ice cover?
4. How do lake-atmosphere interactions differ between daytime and nighttime, monsoon and non-monsoon seasons, ice-covered and ice-free periods, and calm and strong-wind regimes?
5. How do climate warming, changing wind regimes, lake expansion or shrinkage, and reduced ice duration alter lake-atmosphere feedbacks?
6. How well do current weather, climate, hydrological, and land-surface models represent lake processes and lake-atmosphere coupling?

## 3. In Situ Observation Priorities

### 3.1 Long-term lake energy and water balance observations

Future field campaigns should establish long-term lake observation platforms that measure:

- Lake surface temperature and vertical water-temperature profiles.
- Lake mixed-layer depth and thermal stratification.
- Surface radiation components.
- Sensible heat flux and latent heat flux using eddy covariance.
- Wind speed, wind direction, air temperature, humidity, pressure, and precipitation.
- Water level, inflow, outflow, evaporation, and lake storage change.
- Ice phenology, including freeze-up date, break-up date, ice thickness, and snow-on-ice conditions.

These observations are essential for closing lake energy and water budgets and for evaluating lake modules in numerical models.

### 3.2 Lake boundary-layer and turbulence observations

A major knowledge gap is the atmospheric response to lake thermal forcing. Future work should combine lake-platform observations with atmospheric profiling systems, such as:

- Boundary-layer meteorological towers near the lake shore and over open water.
- Doppler wind lidar or wind profiler radar for wind profiles and boundary-layer structure.
- Microwave radiometer for temperature and humidity profiles.
- Radiosonde or tethered-balloon observations during intensive campaigns.
- Scintillometer or distributed temperature sensing for path-averaged turbulent exchange.
- Surface-layer turbulence measurements over both lake and surrounding land.

Such observations can quantify lake-breeze circulation, nighttime instability over warm water, cold-air drainage into basins, and mechanical mixing under strong winds.

### 3.3 Lake-ice-atmosphere observations

For seasonally frozen lakes, future work should focus on the transition periods of freeze-up and break-up. Important measurements include:

- Ice thickness and snow depth on lake ice.
- Thermal conductivity and albedo of ice and snow.
- Under-ice water temperature.
- Heat storage release during freeze-up and early winter.
- Turbulent exchange over snow-covered ice, bare ice, and open-water leads.
- Effects of lake ice on boundary-layer stability and local fog/cloud formation.

These observations are important because lake ice strongly changes surface roughness, albedo, heat flux, evaporation, and lake-effect atmospheric processes.

### 3.4 Lake carbon and aerosol exchange

Lake-atmosphere studies should also include biogeochemical exchange, especially for lakes affected by warming, eutrophication, wetland expansion, or permafrost thaw.

Potential directions include:

- CO2 and CH4 flux measurements using eddy covariance and chamber methods.
- Links between thermal stratification, mixing events, and greenhouse-gas emissions.
- Aerosol production from saline or alkaline lakes.
- Impacts of lake-emitted aerosols on cloud condensation nuclei and local cloud processes.
- Joint analysis of physical, chemical, and biological controls on lake-atmosphere exchange.

## 4. Model Simulation Priorities

### 4.1 Improvement of one-dimensional lake models

One-dimensional lake models are useful for long-term simulation and coupling with land-surface or climate models. Future work should improve:

- Vertical mixing schemes under strong wind and stable stratification.
- Representation of shallow versus deep lakes.
- Lake-ice and snow-on-ice processes.
- Salinity effects on density stratification and freezing temperature.
- Water clarity and radiation penetration.
- Sediment heat storage for shallow lakes.
- Evaporation parameterization under low pressure, high elevation, and strong radiation.

Model evaluation should use multi-depth water temperature, lake surface temperature, ice phenology, turbulent fluxes, and water-level observations.

### 4.2 Coupled lake-atmosphere modeling

Future studies should use coupled atmospheric models with interactive lake modules, such as WRF-Lake or other regional climate models. Key experiments could examine:

- Sensitivity of boundary-layer height to lake surface temperature.
- Lake-breeze intensity under different synoptic backgrounds.
- Effects of lake expansion or shrinkage on local precipitation.
- Impacts of lake heat storage on nighttime and cold-season atmospheric stability.
- Role of lake ice loss in wintertime heat and moisture release.
- Feedbacks between lake evaporation, cloud formation, and regional water recycling.

These simulations should be compared with in situ lake fluxes, atmospheric profiles, satellite lake surface temperature, and precipitation observations.

### 4.3 Large-eddy simulation of lake boundary layers

Large-eddy simulation (LES) can resolve turbulence and lake-breeze structures that are not well represented in mesoscale models. Possible LES topics include:

- Development of convective boundary layers over warm lakes.
- Stable boundary-layer formation over cold lakes.
- Internal boundary layers across land-lake transitions.
- Turbulence response to lake-surface temperature gradients.
- Effects of lake shape, shoreline complexity, and surrounding terrain.
- Coupling between lake breezes and valley/mountain circulations.

LES experiments can guide improved parameterizations for regional and climate models.

### 4.4 Data assimilation and machine learning

Future model studies should also explore data assimilation and machine-learning approaches:

- Assimilation of satellite lake surface temperature into lake models.
- Assimilation of in situ water-temperature profiles and lake level.
- Machine-learning correction of lake-model bias.
- Hybrid physical-ML models for lake evaporation and heat fluxes.
- AI-based detection of lake-breeze events from station, radar, lidar, and satellite data.
- Emulators of computationally expensive coupled lake-atmosphere simulations.

These methods should be used carefully, with physical constraints and independent validation.

## 5. Observation-Model Integration

### 5.1 Process-based field campaigns

Short-term intensive campaigns should be designed around specific processes, such as:

- Spring lake-ice break-up and rapid surface warming.
- Summer monsoon lake-atmosphere coupling.
- Autumn heat release during lake cooling.
- Winter open-water or thin-ice heat flux events.
- Strong-wind mixing events.
- Lake-breeze and land-breeze transitions.

Each campaign should combine lake thermodynamic measurements, turbulent fluxes, atmospheric profiles, cloud and precipitation observations, and model simulations.

### 5.2 Model intercomparison

A coordinated lake-model intercomparison project could test multiple lake models using the same observational dataset. Evaluation metrics should include:

- Lake surface temperature.
- Water-temperature profiles.
- Mixed-layer depth.
- Ice phenology.
- Sensible and latent heat flux.
- Evaporation.
- Boundary-layer response in coupled simulations.

This would help identify structural model uncertainty and guide parameterization improvement.

### 5.3 Scaling from individual lakes to lake clusters

Many regions contain lake clusters rather than isolated lakes. Future work should investigate:

- How lake size distribution affects regional fluxes.
- Whether many small lakes produce cumulative atmospheric effects comparable to one large lake.
- Spatial heterogeneity in lake surface temperature and evaporation.
- Interaction among multiple lake-breeze circulations.
- Effects of lake clusters on regional precipitation and moisture recycling.

This requires combining field observations, satellite remote sensing, and regional modeling.

## 6. Special Topics for High-Altitude and Cold-Region Lakes

High-altitude and cold-region lakes have distinctive characteristics that deserve focused study:

- Low air density and high radiation can alter turbulent exchange.
- Strong diurnal temperature range can produce rapid stability transitions.
- Complex terrain can interact with lake breezes and valley winds.
- Seasonal ice cover strongly changes heat and moisture exchange.
- Lake expansion caused by glacier melt, permafrost thaw, or precipitation change can modify local climate feedbacks.
- Sparse observations make model evaluation difficult.

Possible high-priority questions include:

1. Does lake expansion on high plateaus enhance local evaporation and cloud formation?
2. How does shortened ice-cover duration affect winter and spring atmospheric stability?
3. Can lake heat storage delay boundary-layer cooling in autumn?
4. How do lake breezes interact with mountain-valley circulations?
5. What are the impacts of lake salinity and water clarity on heat storage and evaporation?

## 7. Suggested Future Research Projects

### Project 1: Integrated lake energy balance observation

Establish a lake-platform and shoreline observation network to measure radiation, turbulent fluxes, water temperature, lake level, and meteorological variables for multiple years. Use the dataset to close the lake energy budget and evaluate lake models.

### Project 2: Lake effects on atmospheric boundary-layer structure

Deploy Doppler lidar, microwave radiometer, radiosonde, and turbulence systems around a lake to quantify how the lake modifies boundary-layer height, stability, wind profiles, and local circulation under different seasons and synoptic conditions.

### Project 3: Lake-ice feedback under climate warming

Observe freeze-up, ice growth, snow-on-ice conditions, break-up, and early open-water warming. Use a lake-ice model and coupled atmospheric simulations to assess how shorter ice duration changes winter and spring heat and moisture fluxes.

### Project 4: Coupled modeling of lake expansion impacts

Use satellite-derived historical lake-area changes and coupled regional climate simulations to test how lake expansion affects evaporation, cloudiness, precipitation, and boundary-layer processes.

### Project 5: Lake-atmosphere interaction in complex terrain

Combine LES, mesoscale modeling, and field observations to study the interaction between lake breezes, mountain-valley winds, cold-air drainage, and synoptic flow.

### Project 6: Greenhouse-gas exchange and physical mixing

Measure CO2 and CH4 fluxes together with water-column thermal structure, wind mixing, and atmospheric stability to determine how physical lake-atmosphere exchange controls carbon emissions.

## 8. Recommended Dataset Design

A strong lake-atmosphere dataset should include:

- Multi-year continuous observations.
- At least one full freeze-thaw cycle for cold-region lakes.
- High-frequency turbulence observations over both water and land.
- Vertical water-temperature profiles.
- Atmospheric profile observations.
- Satellite products for spatial context.
- Standardized metadata and quality-control flags.
- Clear documentation of instrument height, depth, sampling interval, calibration, and data gaps.

## 9. Expected Scientific Contributions

Future research combining in situ observation and model simulation can:

- Improve understanding of lake regulation of boundary-layer and local climate processes.
- Reduce uncertainty in lake evaporation and regional water budgets.
- Improve lake modules in weather and climate models.
- Support prediction of lake-effect weather and climate feedbacks.
- Quantify impacts of climate warming and lake change on atmospheric processes.
- Provide observational benchmarks for high-altitude, cold-region, and data-sparse lake systems.
